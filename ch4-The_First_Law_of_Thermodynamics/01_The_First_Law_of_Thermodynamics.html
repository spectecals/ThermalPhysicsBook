This chapter introduces the first law of thermodynamics, the central bookkeeping principle that governs all thermal processes. At its core, the first law expresses energy conservation for thermodynamic systems, linking changes in a system’s internal energy to energy transferred by heat and work. Throughout the chapter, we will sharpen our definitions of heat, work and internal energy, establishing clear sign conventions used in subsequent derivations. This notational framework will allow us to analyse physical processes consistently, regardless of the complexity of the path between states.

Building on this foundation, this chapter will explore how different thermodynamic processes - such as cyclic, isothermal, isochoric, isobaric and adiabatic transformations - emerge from the first law when specific constraints are imposed. We then examine heat capacities, showing how they quantify a system’s response to heating and lead naturally to the gas constant \(R\) and the adiabatic index \(\gamma\). These ideas culminate in the derivation of the adiabatic equation of state, \(pV^{\gamma} = \text{const}\), revealing how microscopic energy storage shapes macroscopic gas behaviour.

We will begin by reviewing the concepts of work and internal energy.

4.1. 4.1 Review: Work#

The conservation of energy is fundamental to thermodynamics because it governs how energy is transferred and transformed within a system. It provides the basis for the first law of thermodynamics, which relates changes in internal energy to heat added to a system and work done by the system. By applying energy conservation, we can distinguish between energy stored internally and energy exchanged as work or heat, enabling a consistent understanding of thermodynamic processes and their limits.

Work:#

Work is done by exerting a force on an object, whilst the object is moved from one position to another (or has its shape warped). There is a displacement of the object (see figure below):

[FIGURE 6.2 HERE]

Work measures the energy transferred by a force acting through a process of displacement. When an object moves a distance \(s\) in a straight line under a constant force \(F\) which acts in the same direction as the displacement, the work done is calculated to be simply the product of \(F\) and \(s\):

\[ W = F s \tag{4.1} \]
\[ \text{[Joules, J]} = \text{[Newtons, N]} \cdot \text{[Displacement, m]} \]

From the equation above we can see that work can be increased by amplifying the force or by applying the same force over a greater distance.

Sign of work:#

The sign of the work depends on the relative angle of the force to the object that is being displaced. The figure below helps to illustrate how this works:

[FIGURE 6.3 HERE]

With vector representations for the quantities involved, \(\vec{F}\) & \(\vec{s}\) we can produce two other equivalent statements for work:

\[ W = |\vec{F}||\vec{s}| \ \cos(\phi) = Fs \ \cos(\phi), \tag{4.2} \]

where \(\phi\) is the angle between \(\vec{F}\) and \(\vec{s}\). If \(\phi=0\), so that \(\vec{F}\) and \(\vec{s}\) act in the same direction, then \(\cos(\phi)=1\) and we regain Eq. (4.1).

Eq. (4.2) has the form of a scalar product of two vectors, meaning it can be equivalently represented as a dot product of two vectors:

\[ W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s}. \tag{4.3} \]

Work can take any real value, from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\), but it is a scalar quantity: it has magnitude but no intrinsic direction. This is true despite work being defined in terms of vector quantities. A 5 N force acting eastward on an object that then moves 6 m east does the same amount of work as a 5 N force acting northward on an object which then moves 6 m north.

The sign of the work encodes the direction of energy transfer between the system and its surroundings. Once a sign convention is fixed, positive and negative values of work correspond to different physical situations. In this convention, positive work represents energy transferred to the system by the surroundings, while negative work represents energy transferred from the system to the surroundings. The mathematical form of the first law remains unchanged; only the interpretation of the sign differs.

5.1. The work-energy theorem#

The total work done on an object by external forces is linked to its displacement, but its physical importance lies in how it changes the object’s speed. Work is positive when the net force has a component in the direction of motion, negative when it opposes the motion, and zero when the net force lies perpendicular to the displacement (or when no force is applied, of course).

The effect of work on motion:

  • Positive work \(\rightarrow\) Increases the object’s speed.

  • Negative work \(\rightarrow\) Decreases the object’s speed.

  • Zero work \(\rightarrow\) The object’s speed is unchanged.

Thus, work provides a direct quantitiative measure of how forces modify motion. The kinetic energy of a particle is defined as:

\[ K = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}, \tag{4.4} \]
\[ \text{[Joules, J]} = \text{[Mass, kg]} \cdot \text{[Velocity, m/s]}^2 \]

where \(m\) is the mass of said particle, and \(v\) is its’ velocity. Like work, kinetic energy is a scalar quantity, independent of the direction of motion; although in this case it is always non negative (\(K \geq 0\)), only becoming zero when the particle is at rest.

For motion along a straight line under a constant net force, combining Newton’s second law with kinematics shows that the work done by the net force over a displacement equals the difference between the final and initial kinetic energies:

\[ W_{tot} = K_{final} - K_{initial} = \Delta K. \tag{4.5} \]

This is obtained via the following derivation:

\[ v_{f}^2 = v_{i}^2 + 2as, \]

where \(v_f\), \(v_i\) denote the final and intial particle velocity, whilst a denotes the acceleration corresponding to the applied force (\(F=ma\)). We can rearrange the equation above to get an expression for the displacement:

\[ s = \frac{v_{f}^2 - v_{i}^2}{2a}. \]

The work of the net force is the product of its magnitute (\(F=ma\)) and the particle’s displacement (assuming the vectors \(\vec{F}\) and \(\vec{s}\) are parallel):

\[\begin{split} \begin{align*} W = Fs &= ma \frac{v_f^2 - v_i^2}{2a} = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_f^2 - \tfrac{1}{2} m v_i^2 \\ &= K_f - K_i = \Delta K \end{align*} \end{split}\]

This theorem states that the total work done by all forces acting on a particle simply equals its overall change in kinetic energy. If the net work is positive, \(\Delta K\) is also positive and the particle speeds up. The reverse is true if work is negative, and of course if work is zero then the particle’s speed is unchanged even though forces may be acting. Although derived for a straight-line motion with constant forces, the work-energy theorem is valid for more general motions and force laws, provided the analysis is carried out in an intertial reference frame.

Note that if an object is displaced from rest (e.g. a billiard ball), then the work-energy theorem is simply:

\[ W = K_2 - 0 = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2. \]

5.2. Work and energy with varying forces:#

Above, work was defiend for constant forces. In many physical situatuions however, the force varies in magnitude and/or direction along the path of motion. The work-energy theorem remains valid even in these cases.

Consider motion along the x-axis, where the x-component of the force, \(F_x(x)\), varies with position. We can divide the displacement from \(x_1\) to \(x_2\) into small segments \(\Delta x\), where we can linearly approximate the work done over each segment:

\[ \Delta W = Fx \Delta x; \]

this would allow us to approximate the total work by summing over each of these segments…

\[ W = Fx_1 \Delta x_1 + Fx_2 \Delta x_2 + ... \]

The exact expression is given in integral form, in the limit of \(\Delta x \rightarrow 0\):

\[ W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} F_x(x) dx. \tag{4.6} \]

Graphically, this work equals the area under the \(F_x(x)\) curve between \(x_1\) and \(x_2\). For a constant force \(F_x\), this reduces to \(W = F_xx(x_2 - x_1)\).

Spring Force (Hooke’s Law):

For an ideal spring stretched or compressed along \(x\), \(F_x = kx\) where \(k\) is the spring constant.

What is the work being done by stretching a spring from \(x=0\) to \(x\)?

\[ W = \int_0^x F_x dx = \int_0^x kx dx = tfrac{1}{2} kx^2 \]

[SET OF FIGURES HERE IF YOU HAVE TIME]

The work done between the two positions \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) is:

\[ W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} F_x dx = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} kx dx = tfrac{1}{2} kx_2^2 - tfrac{1}{2} kx_1^2 \]

[SECOND HALF OF THE FIGURES FOR THIS EXAMPLE]

Therefore, the work-energy theorem indeed holds for scenarios where the driving force varies with position.

We can also derive this outcome by considering a calculus approach. The acceleration of a particle can be described using the chain rule:

\[ a_x = \frac{dv_x}{dt} = \frac{dv_x}{dx}\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{dv_x}{dx}v_x \]

Using Newton’s second law to write \(F_x = ma_x\), the total work is given by:

\[ W_{tot} = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} F_x dx = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} ma_x dx = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} mv_x \frac{dv_x}{dx}dx \]

The \(dx\) in the numerator and denominator cancel out, making leaving the integration with respect to velocity:

\[ W_{tot} = \int_{v_1}^{v_2} mv_x dv_x = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_2^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}mv_1^2 \]

which yields the same conclusion as Eq. (4.5). Therefore, the work-energy theorem is valid for straight-line motion even when the net force varies with position, and does not rely on the assumption of constant force.

We can also extend the work-energy theorem for motion along a curve, in which a force varies in direction as well as magnitude, and thus the displacement lies along a curved path. Consider the scenario depicted in the figure below:

[FIGURE 6.23]

For a particle moving from point \(P_1\) to \(P_2\) along a curve:

  • Divide the path into infinitesmal vector displacements \(dl\), each tangential to the path.

  • Let \(F\) be the force at a point along the path.

  • Only the component of the force parallel to the displacement does work.

The differential work is:

\[ dW = \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{l} = F\cos(\phi) dl = F_{\parallel}dl \]

Phi is the angle between \(F\) and \(dl\), and \(F_{\parallel} = Fcos(\phi)\) is the force component along the path. The total work done as the particle moves from P1 to P2 is therefore:

\[ W = \int_{P_1}^{P_2} = \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{l} = \int_{P_1}^{P_2} F\cos(\phi) \ dl = \int_{P_1}^{P_2} F_{\parallel} dl. \tag{4.7} \]

This is a line integral, and it accounts for both the changing direction of motion and the changing force along the path. The work-energy theorem holds generally - by summing up the infintesimal work \(dW = Fdl\) over all segments of a curved path, the total work equals the total change in kinetic energy \(W_{tot} = \Delta K = K_2 - K_1\), regardless of how the force varies.

Only the component of the force parallel to the path does work and changes the particle’s speed, while the perpendicular component changes only in the direction of motion.

5.3. 4.2 Review: Internal Energy#

\(\underline{\text{Conservation forces:}}\) The work done by a conservative force has the following characteristics:

  1. It can be written as the difference between the final and initial values of a potential energy function.

  2. The work is reversible: reversing the motion reverses the sign of the work.

  3. The work is independent of the parth taken and depends only on the starting and ending points.

  4. The total work done over any closed path is zero. (A closed path is a path that returns to its initial state.)

When only conservative forces do work, the total mechanical energy (\(E=K+U\)) is conserved. Examples include the gravitational force, spring force and the electric force.

\(\underline{\text{Nonconservative forces:}}\) Not all forces are conservative. Friction and fluid resistance do negative work regardless of the direction of motion, so the work done over a closed path is not zero and the lost kinetic energy cannot be recovered; hence, mechanical energy is not conserved and no potential energy can be defined for these forces. Such forces are categorised as being nonconservative, and many are dissipative, converting mechanical energy into other forms. Some nonconservative forces, like explosive or chemical forces, can increase mechanical energy in irreversible processes.

In other words, nonconservative forces can either dissipate mechanical energy (like friction) or generate mechanical energy by converting other forms of energy (like chemical potential) into motion - in both cases, the process cannot be undone.

\(\underline{\text{Conservation of Energy:}}\) Non-conservative forces, such as friction, cannot be described by a potential energy function. However, their effects do not violate the law of conservation of energy. To account for them, we introduce the concept of internal energy, denoted, \(U_{int}\).

Internal energy is the energy associated with the microscopic motion and interactions of the particles which make up a system. Increasing the temperature of a system increases its internal energy; decreasing the temperature lowers it.

Consider a system on which friction does negative work, such as a block sliding across a rough surface. The system slows down, so its kinetic energy decreases. However, experiments show that this “lost” mechanical energy has not disappeared. Instead, it is converted into internal energy and its surroundings. Quantitatively, this is expressed as:

\[ \Delta U_{int} = -W_{other}, \tag{4.8} \]

where \(W_{other}\) is the work done by non-conservative forces (such as friction) and \(\Delta U_{int}\) is the resulting change in internal energy. We can now write the total energy of the system as the sum of its kinetic energy \(K\), potential energy \(P\), and internal energy \(U_{int}\). The law of the conservation of energy requires that the total energy remains constant:

\[ K_1 + P_1 + U_{int,1} = K_2 + P_2 + U_{int,2}. \]

Rearranging and rewriting this in terms of differentials (e.g. \(\Delta K - K_2 - K_1\)) explicitly yields:

\[ \Delta K + \Delta P + \Delta U_{int} = 0. \tag{4.9} \]

This is the general statement of energy conservation: although energy may be transferred between kinetic, potential and internal forms, the total energy of a closed system is conserved. A useful consequence follows immediately; if no energy is transferred into internal energy (for example, when only conservative forces act) then \(\Delta U_{int} = 0\), and the familiar result

\[ \Delta K + \Delta P = 0 \tag{4.10} \]

is recovered. In this special case, mechanical energy is conserved.

5.4. 4.3 Work done during volume changes#

In thermodynamics, work is defined generally as energy transferred across a system boundary by any means other than heat. Expressions such as force multiplied by displacement arise as special cases of this definition when analysing mechanical systems, but it can be considered more generally as part of the thermodynamic framework.

Now lets take the concept of work and apply it to a thermodynamic system (any collection of objects or matter that is conveniently treated as a single unit for analysis, and that may exchange energy with its surroundings).

[FIGURE 19.3]

Consider a simple example of a thermodynamics system, a gas enclosed in a cylinder with a moveable piston. This system is coming in engines (e.g. internal combustion) and compressors (e.g. in refrigerators).

[FIGURE 19.4]

When a system such as a gas changes volume against a moveable piston, it can do work on its surroundings. An expanding gas pushes the piston outward and does positive work, whilst when the piston moves inward and the gas is compressed, work is done on the gas (this can also be thought of as the gas doing negative work). At the molecular level, work only occurs when gas molecules collide with a moving piston, not a stationary wall.

[FIGURE 19.5]

See the figure above, which shows a system (gas, liquid, solid) in a hollow cylinder with one end enclosed with a moveable piston. The piston has a cross-sectional surface are equivalent to the cylinder (\(A\)), and the enclosed gas exerts a pressure \(p\) on the piston face. The total force exerted on the piston is \(F=pA\). If in response to this force the piston moves an infinitesimal distance \(dx\), the work done in this process is:

\[ dW = F \ dx = pA \ dx. \tag{4.11} \]

Since \(Adx = dV\), where dV is the infinitesimal volume change of the system, the work done by the system in this infinitesimal volume change is simply re-expressed as:

\[ dW = p \ dV. \tag{4.12} \]

In a finite change of volume, \(V_1\) to \(V_2\):

\[ W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} p dV. \tag{4.13} \]

During a volume change, the system pressure generally varies, so evaluating Eq. (4.13) requires knowledge of the pressure-volume relationship \(p(V)\). This work is represented graphically on a pV-diagram (see Chapter 3) as the area under the \(p(V)\) curve between the points \(V_1\) and \(V_2\).

For expansion (\(V_2 > V_1\), the area is positive and the system does positive work on its surroundings. For a compression (\(V_2 < V_1\)), the area is negative and the system does negative work. In the special case where pressure is constant throughout the whole process, Eq. (4.13) reduces to:

\[ W = p(V_2 - V_1). \tag{4.14} \]

For situations where the volume is constant, \(dV = 0\) and hence no work is done.

5.5. 4.4 Work and heat in a thermodynamic process#

We now consider how the work performed by a system and the heat transferred to it during a thermodynamic process depend on the specific path and conditions under which the process occurs.

To understand intuitively how a path can influence the work performed by a system, picture how the difficulty rating of hiking trails which all lead to the same destination can challenge you differently, requiring more or less work on your part depending on the journey.

When a thermodynamic system described using pressure and volume (\(p\), \(V\)), evolves from an initial state (\(p_1\), \(V_1\)) to a final state (\(p_2\), \(V_2\)), it passes through a sequence of intermediate equilibrium states called a path. Infinitely many paths can connect the same initial and final states on a pV-diagram, in the same manner in which two points on a graph can be connected via infinitely many curves.

Paths from state 1 to state 2

  • Path 1 \(\rightarrow\) 3 \(\rightarrow\) 2

    • 1 \(\rightarrow\) 3: Constant pressure expansion \(p_1\) to volume \(V_2\) \(\rightarrow\) work done equals the area under the curve.

    • 3 \(\rightarrow\) 2: The pressure decreases however the volume stays constant \(\rightarrow\) no work is done.

  • Path 1 \(\rightarrow\) 3 \(\rightarrow\) 2

    • 1 \(\rightarrow\) 4: Constant volume process \(\rightarrow\) no work done.

    • 4 \(\rightarrow\) 2: Expansion at varying pressure \(\rightarrow\) work equals the area under the segment.

  • Path 1 \(\rightarrow\) 4 (smooth curve)

    • Continuous variation of pressure with volume \(\rightarrow\) the work differs from both piecewise paths preceding.

Like work, heat transfer in a thermodunamic process is path dependent. For example, consider an isothermal expansion (Section XYZ, constant temperature) of an ideal gas:

Initial state: \(V_1=2.0 \ L\), \(T=300 \ K\)

Final state: \(V_2=5.0 \ L\), \(T=300 \ K\)

  • Path A (quasi-static isothermal expansion with a piston): The gas expands slowly against a movable piston while heat is supplied to maintain constant temperature. The gas absorbs a definite, nonzero amount of heat and does work.

  • Path B (free expansion into a vacuum): The gas rapidly expands rapidly into an evacuated region inside an isulated container. No work is done and no heat is transferred. For an ideal gas, the temperature remains unchanged, so the final state is identical as in the case of Path A.

Although the final and initial state are the same, the heat transferred and work done are different because the intermediate states (the path) differ.

Key Conclusion: Heat and work are path-dependent quantities, so they cannot be assigned definite values for a given thermodynamic state and it is meaningless to say that a system “contains” heat or work. In contrast, internal energy is a state function, uniquely determined by the system’s state, which is why it plays a central and fundamental role in thermodynamics.

5.7. 4.5 The first law of thermodynamics#

It may seem obvious to us today that heat and work represent forms of transient energy, but this understanding came about over a period of gradual discoveries. In the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, heat was not initially regarded as a form of energy at all. Instead, it was treated as a material substance. The eventual recognition of heat and work as equivalent forms of energy was a major conceptual breakthrough that laid the foundation for the modern interpretation of thermodynamics.

1789 - Antoine Lavoisier: Proposes the caloric theory, transferring heat as a conserved, weightless fluid responsible for thermal phenomena and combustion - (https://doi.org/10.1017/S000708740001195X).

1798 - Benjamin Thompson (count Rumford): Demonstrates that friction during cannon boring can generate seemingly unlimited heat, contradicting the idea that caloric is a conserved substance - (https://doi.org/10.1098/rstl.1798.0006).

1942 - Julius Robert Mayer: Proposes a quantitative relationship between mechanical work and heat, arguing that both are manifestations of a conserved quantity - energy - ().

1840-1845 - James Prescott Joule: Performs precise experiments (including paddle-wheel and electrical heating) to measure the mechanical equivalent of heat, showing that a fixed amount of work always produces the same amount of heat - (https://doi.org/10.1098/rstl.1850.0004).

Outcome: Heat came to be recognised as a form of energy rather than a material substance, leading to the abandonment of caloric theory and the formulation of the first law of thermodynamics, often summarised as:

\[ \text{"Energy is conserved and heat and work are both forms of energy."} \]

Internal Energy#

A thermodynamic system is characterised by its internal energy, denoted \(U\). Internal energy represents the total energy associated with the system’s internal degrees of freedom; this includes:

  • The combined kinetic energy of the constituent particles (Translational, Rotational, Vibrational).

  • The potential enegies arising from interactions between those particles.

The internal energy is a state function: its value depends only on the current state of the system, not on how that state was reached. Therefore, it contains no information about whether the energy encapsulated within the systen came from heat or work.

Heat and work#

The internal energy of a system can only be changed by transferring energy across the system boundary. In thermodynamics, there are two distinct modes of energy transfer:

  • Heat \(Q\): energy transferred due to a temperature difference.

  • Work \(W\): energy transferred by all other mechanisms.

Heat and work are not state functions. Their values depend on the process by which the system evolves between states. Once a process is complete, the final state of the system contains no information about how much of the energy change arose from heat transfer and how much arose from work.

Sign Conventions and the First Law#

The first law of thermodynamics expresses conservation of energy for a thermodynamic system by relating changes in internal energy to heat and work.

Different sign conventions are used in the literature to define work. To avoid confusion, we will state both conventions explicitly before selecting one to proceed with for the remainder of the course.

If work is defined as being positive when work is done by the system, \(W = W_{by}\) and the first law may be written as:

\[ \Delta U = Q - W_{\text{by}}, \tag{4.15} \]

where:

  • \(Q > 0\) corresponds to heat added to the system. If it’s negative then heat is lost.

  • \(W_{\text{by}} > 0\) corresponds to work done by the system on its surroundings.

Alternatively, if work is defined as positive when done on the system, \(W=W_{on}\), and the first law may be written as:

\[ \Delta U = Q + W_{\text{on}}, \tag{4.16} \]

with $\( W_{\text{on}} = -\, W_{\text{by}}. \)$

Both expressions describe the same physical principle - energy conservation - and differ only by the chosen sign convention.

Throughout this course, we adopt the convention that work is positive when it is done ON the system.

Accordingly, we will write the first law using Eq. (4.16), with:

  • \(Q > 0\) when heat is added to the system,

  • \(Q < 0\) when heat is removed from the system,

  • \(W > 0\) when work is done on the system,

  • \(W < 0\) when work is done by the system.

When further clarification is warranted, we will expliticly make use of the labels: \(Q_{added}\), \(Q_{removed}\), \(W_{on}\), \(W_{by}\).

Cyclic Processes:#

[FIGURE OF A CYCLIC PROCESS GRAPHED]

A cyclic process is a thermodynamic process in which a system evolves through a sequence of states and eventually returns to its initial state. On a pV-diagram, a cyclic process appears as a closed loop.

Because the system ends in the same state in which it began, all state variables return to their original values. In particular, the internal energy at the end of the cycle is equal to the internal energy at the beginning, so the resulting change in internal energy over a complete cycle is therefore zero.

\[ U_2 = U_1 \therefore \Delta U = U_2 - U_1 = 0. \]

Notably, the temperature of the system also goes unchanged. Using the first law of thermodynamics:

\[ \Delta U = Q \ + \ W = Q_{added} + W_{on}, \]

we find that for a cyclic process,

\[ 0 = Q \ + \ W, \]

or equivalently,

\[ Q_{added} \ = \ - W_{on} \ = \ W_{by} \]

This means that, over a complete cycle, the net heat added to the system is equal in magnitude and opposite in sign to the net work done on the system. Neither the heat nor work needs to be zero individually, only their sum must vanish.

The work appearing in the first law is defined as the work done on the system. However, on a pV-diagram, the mechanical work is given by the integral

\[ W_{by} = \int p dV, \tag{4.17} \]

which represents the work done by the gas. Therefore to make this suitable to our convention, we slightly amend Eq. (4.17) by multiplying the integral by -1, yielding:

\[ W_{on} = - \int p dV. \]

With this expression, compression (\(V_2 < V_1\)) corresponds to positive \(W\), and expansion (\(V_2 > V_1\)) corresponds to negative \(W\). When computing the integral of a cyclic process, the limits of integration form a closed loop and we write the work done on the gas as:

\[ W = - \oint p dV. \tag{4.18} \]

The sign of the integral itself depends on the direction in which the loop is traversed on the pV-diagram.

  • A clockwise cycle corresponds to a positive value of \(\oint p dV\), resulting in an overall negative value. In this case, work is done by the gas on its surroundings.

  • A counter-clockwise cycle corresponds to a negative value of \(\oint p dV\), hence (through a double-negative), the work is positive meaning work is done on the system.

Cyclic processes are fundamental to the operation of heat engines. In a heat engine, the system absorbs heat from a hot reservoir and performs net work on its surroundings over each cycle, while its internal energy remains unchanged.

Do note, in an isolated system, there is is no heat exchange carried out between said system and it’s surroundings, as well as no work done on or by the surroundings, therefore \(Q = W = 0\). Consequently, the internal energy remains constant - thus we conclude that the internal energy of an isolated system does not change during any process.

5.8. 4.6 Thermodynamic Processes#

Now let us consider various cases for gas expansion…

1 - Adiabatic Process:#

An adiabatic process is one in which there is no heat transfer into or out of the system (hence, \(Q=0\)). Therefore, in adiabatic processes the changes is temperature are brought about entirely through work, and not by heat transfer. Practically speaking, this can be achieved through sufficient system isolation; or by completing the process sufficiently quick such that heat does not have enough time to flow:

\[ \Delta U = W_{on} = - W_{by}\ \ \text{(adiabatic process)} \]

During adiabatic expansion, the volume of the system is increased without allowing for heat transfer. Expansion means work done is being done by the system, which can be interpreted as negative work done on the system. Therefore the change in internal energy is negative and the temperature is typically also reduced.

During adiabatic compression, the volume of the system decreases without allowing for heat transfer. Compression means that the work done on the system is positive; hence the change in internal energy reflects this which typically corresponds to an increase in the system’s temperature.

2 - Isobaric Process:#

An isobaric process is one in which the pressure remains constant. Such processes commonly occur when a gas is allowed to expand or contract against a constant external pressure; consider a gas beneath a freely moving piston, or boiling water exposed to atmospheric pressure.

On a p-V diagram, an isobaric process is represented by a horizontal line, since the pressure does not change whilst the volume increases or decreases.

  • Initial state: (\(p\), \(V_i\))

  • Final state: (\(p\), \(V_f\))

The area under this horizontal line corresponds to the work done by the gas. Simply multiply the difference between the final and initial volume by the constant pressure, and minus one:

\[ W = W_{on} = -p (V_f - V_i) = - W_{by} \tag{4.19} \]

In an isobaric process, none of the quantities \(\Delta U\), \(Q\) or \(W\) are necessarily zero. The first law still applies as normal. Unlike in the to-be-explained isothermal process, the temperature generally changes, so the internal energy also changes.

  • During isobaric expansion, heat added to the gas is shared between: (1) increasing internal energy (raising temperature) and (2), doing work on the surroundings.

  • During isobaric compression, work is done on the gas and heat must typically be removed.

3 - Isothermal Process#

An isothermal process is one which occurs at constant temperature, \(\Delta T = 0\). For a process to remain isothermal, heat must flow into or out of the system slowly enough that thermal equilibrium with the surroundings is maintained at all times. In practice, this requires good thermal contact with a heat reservoir and quasi-static motion of the piston.

For many systems, internal energy depends on both temperature and volume. However, for an ideal gas, the internal energy depends only on temperature. Therefore in an isothermal process involving an ideal gas, \(\Delta U = 0\). This is a special but extremely important case.

Starting from the first law:

\[ \Delta U = Q_{added} + W_{on} \]
\[ 0 = Q_{added} + W_{on} \]
\[ Q_{added} = - W_{on} = W_{by} \]

Therefore all the heat added to the system is converted directly into work done by the gas. No energy gets stored internally. Now let us consider a gas in a cylinder with a movable piston:

[DIAGRAM OF OF A CYLINDER WITH A MOVEABLE PISTON LID]

Initially, the gas has volume \(V_1\) and temperature \(T_1\). As the piston moves outward, the gas expands and does work on the surroundings. Expansion by itself would cool the gas. To keep the temperature constant, heat must flow into the gas from the surroundings.

(Reminder: a compressed gas tends to heat up and an expanding gas tends to cool down. This implies that heat must be added during isothermal expansion to maintain a constant temperature.)

So what is the work done by the gas? Let’s consult a pV-diagram…

[GIVE IT UP FOR ANOTHER PV DIAGRAM]

The isothermal path follows a curve defined by \(pV=nRT\) in which \(T\) is constant. The pressure decreases smoothly as volume increases. The area under the curve represents the work done by the gas. Compared to an adiabatic expansion from the same initial point, the isothermal curve is less steep, because heat input prevents the temperature from dropping. The work done by the gas is given by:

\[ W_{by} = \int_i^f pdV, \]

using the ideal gas law,

\[ p = \frac{nRT}{V}. \]

We can sub this expression for \(p\) into the integral:

\[\begin{split} \begin{align} W_{by} &= nRT \int_i^f \frac{1}{V} dV \\ &= nRT \ \left[ \ln(V) \right]_i^f \\ &= nRT \ \ln\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i} \right) \end{align} \tag{4.20} \end{split}\]

Where \(W_{by} = Q_{added}\). In summary, in an ideal isothermal system, the temperature remains constant (\(\Delta T = 0\)), the internal energy change is zero (\(\Delta U = 0\)), and the heat added is equal to the work done by the gas.

Gas expansion and compression

1 mol (\(n=1\)) of gas is kept at zero degrees celcius (273 K) during an expansion from 3 L to 10 L.

(a) How much work is done by the gas? (Isothermal expansion step)

\[\begin{split} \begin{align} W_{by} &= nRT ln\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i} \right) \\ &= 1 \times 8.31 \times 273 \times ln \left(\tfrac{10}{3}\right) \\ &= 2.7 \times 10^3 \ J \end{align} \end{split}\]

(b) How much heat is transferred into the system in the process?

Remember that for isothermal processes, \(Q_{added} = W_{by}\), so this quantity is also \(2.7 \times 10^3 \ J\), as all of the heat is converted into work.

(c) How much work is done to return the gas to its original volume via an Isobaric process?

\[\begin{split} \begin{align} W_{by} &= p (V_f - V_i) \\ &= \frac{nRT}{V_i} (V_f - V_i) \\ &= \frac{1 \times 8.31 \times 273}{10\times 10^{-3}} (3\times 10^{-3} - 10\times 10^{-3}) \\ &= - 1.6\times 10^{3} \end{split}\]

The negative sign here indicates that work is being done ON the gas during compression.

5.9. 4.7 Cloud Formation (Key example)#

Cloud formation provides a useful real-world example of an adiabatic process, recalling that ‘adiabatic’ means no heat is transferred (\(Q = 0\)). Consider moist air being forced up the side of a mountain by the wind, illustrated in the figure below.

[FIGURE OF WATER VAPOUR RISING OVER A MOUNTAIN]

As the air rises, it moves into regions of lower atmospheric pressure, brought on at greater altitudes because there is less air from above pushing down due to gravity. To remain in pressure equilibrium with its surroundings, the rising air must expand. During this expansion the air does work on its surroundings (i.e. work is being done by the gas). Because the air is not in any meaningful thermal contact with the mountain surface, this expansion occurs with negligible heat exchange and can be treated as being adiabatic.

From the first law of thermodynamics, an adiabatic expansion leads to a decrease in internal energy which corresponds to a decrease in the gas pocket’s temperature. This cooling is an essential process for cloud formation.

Partial Pressure versus vapour pressure#

Air is a mixture of gases, including Nitrogen, Oxygen, and water vapour, amongst small amounts of other gases, such as Argon, methane and of course Carbon Dioxide. Each component contributes to the total pressure of the composite gas. The partial pressure of a constituent gas is the pressure that gas would exert if it alone occupied the entire volume of the combined, mixture gas.

For example, if air at a total pressure of 100 kPa contains 2% water vapour, then the partial pressure of the water vapour is

\[ P_{water} = \left( \frac{V_{water}}{V_{total}} \right) p_{total} = 100 \ \text{kPa} \times 2\% = 2 \ \text{kPa}. \]

The vapour pressure of water is different. It is the maximum pressure that water vapour can exert at a given temperature before condensation occurs. Vapour pressure depends strongly on the temperature. Cooler air can hold less water vapour.

As the rising air cools during adiabatic expansion, the vapour pressure of the air decreases. Cloud formation begins when the partial pressure of water becomes equal to the vapour pressure. If the air cools further (e.g. by gaining additional elevation), the partial pressure will exceed the vapour pressure, and the excess water must condense into liquid droplets. These droplets scatter light and appear as the clouds we see in the sky.

  • When partial pressure > vapour pressure \(\rightarrow\) condensation \(\rightarrow\) clouds are formed.

  • When partial pressure < vapour pressure \(\rightarrow\) water remains a vapour.

[FIGURE OF PRESSURE VERSUS TEMPERATURE]

Vapour Pressure Example

Air can hold up to 0.01% of its volume in vapour form at -42°C and 4.2% of its volume at 30°C. The partial pressure of water at 42°C is 3%, what is the relative humidity?

\[ \text{Relative Humidity:} = \frac{partial pressure}{vapour pressure} = \frac{3\% \times V}{4.2\% \times V} = 71.4\% \tag{4.21} \]

If the partial pressure is greater than the vapour pressure, then we have 100% relative humidity.

5.10. 4.8 Internal Energy of an ideal gas: Free Expansion#

In a free expansion, a gas expands into a vacuum inside an insulated container. No heat enters or leaves the system and the gas does no work: \(Q = W = 0\) thus \(\Delta U = 0\). Does the temperature of the gas change?

Suppose that the temperature did change during free expansion whilst the internal energy stayed constant. We would then have to conlcude that the internal energy depends not only on temperature \(T\), but also on volume \(V\) or pressure \(p\). However, experiments show that for a low-density (ideal) gas, the temperature does not change during free expansion, despite the fact that \(V\) and \(p\) do. This leads us to a crucial conclusion:

For an ideal gas, the internal energy, \(U\), depends only on temperature \(T\), not on pressure or volume.

This property, together with the ideal-gas equations of state is a fundamental part of the ideal-gas model and will be used repeatedly. For non-ideal gases, experiments do show a temperature change during free expansion despite an unchanging internal energy (\(\Delta U = 0\)). This means that internal energy cannot entirely depend on temperature; it must also depend on pressure and/or volume. Considering the microscopic reality of gases, this result is not surprising. Non-ideal gases have intermoleculer forces; as molecules move further apart from each other during expansion the experience an increase in their potential energies. If total internal energy remains constant the kinetic energy must drop accordingly - corresponding to a drop in temperature.

5.11. 4.9 Heat capacitites for an ideal gas#

To understand how heat affects a gas’s internal energy, note that, in general, the internal energy depends on both temperature and volume - \(U = U(V, T)\). Therefore a small change in internal energy can be expressed in terms of small changes in temperature and volume.

\[ dU = \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_V dT + \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_T dV. \tag{4.22} \]

Note that:

\[ dU = dQ + dW, \]

and

\[ dW = - p dV. \]

Therefore rearranging the first law using \(dW = - pdV\) yields:

\[ dQ = dU + pdV, \]

and now using eq. (4.22) we have that:

\[ dQ = \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_V dT + \left[ \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_T + p \right] dV. \tag{4.23} \]

We then divide eq. (4.23) by dT to obtain:

\[ \frac{dQ}{dT} = \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_V + \left[ \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_T + p \right] \frac{dV}{dT}. \]

This relationship holds for any changes in temperature or volume, but our goal is to determine how much heat myst be added to change the temperature under specific constaints. The first case is when the volume is held constant, which leads to the definition of heat capacity at constant volume, \(C_V\), which describes the heat required to raise the temperature of a system without changing its volume.

\[ C_V = \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial T} \right)_V = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_V \tag{4.24} \]

The heat capacity at constant pressure is then given by:

\[ C_p = \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial T} \right)_p = \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_V + \left[ \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_T + p \right] \left( \frac{dV}{dT} \right)_p, \tag{4.25} \]

such that:

\[ C_p - C_V = \left[ \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_T + p \right] \left( \frac{dV}{dT} \right)_p. \tag{4.26} \]

Recall that the heat capacity has units of \(\left[ \text{J} \cdot \text{K}^{-1} \right]\) and refers to a specific amount of gas. It is often practical to express heat capacity per mole or per unit mass; the lower is denoted using a lowercase ‘c’ and is referred to as the specific heat capacity.

\[ c_V = \frac{C_V}{M}, \tag{4.27} \]
\[ c_p = \frac{C_p}{M}, \tag{4.28} \]

where \(M\) is the mass of the material. Specific heat capacities have units of \(\left[ \text{J} \cdot \text{K}^{-1} \cdot \text{kg}^{-1} \right]\).

Let us now put this into practice for a monatomic gas. For an ideal monatomic gas, the internal energy arises solely from its constituents kinetic energy and equals \(U = \tfrac{3}{2}RT\) per mole. Consequently, the internal energy depends only on the temperature. Hence:

\[ \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_T = 0. \]

The equation of state for 1 mole of an ideal gas is simply \(pV = RT\). Rearranging to make \(V\) the subject on the LHS and differentiating with respect to \(T\) at constant pressure gives us:

\[ \left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial T} \right)_p = \frac{R}{p}. \]

Supplying this result and its predecessor to eq. (4.26) we have that

\[ C_p - C_V = \left[ \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial V} \right)_T + p \right] \left( \frac{dV}{dT} \right)_p = R. \]

Because \(U = \tfrac{3}{2}RT\), we therefore have that:

\[ C_V = \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_V = \frac{3}{2}R \ \text{per mole}, \tag{4.29} \]

and

\[ C_p = C_V + R = \frac{5}{2}R \ \text{per mole}. \tag{4.30} \]

The ratio of \(C_p\) and \(C_V\) is a very important and useful quantity as named the adiabtatic index (\(\gamma\)):

\[ \gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_V}. \tag{4.31} \]

For an ideal monatomic gas:

\[ \gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_V} = \frac{C_V + R}{C_V} = 1 + \frac{R}{C_V} = \frac{5}{3}. \]

5.12. 4.10 pV\(^{\gamma}\) for adiabatic processes (Derivation for pV\(^{\gamma}\) = constant)#

For a perfect ideal gas, we have that \(pV^{\gamma}\) is constant for all adiabatic processes (recall that adiabatic refers to contexts in which heat transfer (\(Q\)) is zero). This does not mean that each of the parameters \(p\), \(V\) and \(\gamma\) are constant, we instead find that \(\Delta p\), \(\Delta V\) and \(\Delta \gamma\) are all non-zero, but their resulting values combine to reform the constant \(pV^{\gamma}\).

We will prove that \(pV^{\gamma}\) is a constant. Starting with the internal energy of an ideal gas:

\[ U = \tfrac{3}{2}nRT, \]

we differentiate both sides with respect to temperature (\(T\)), then multiply both sides by \(dT\)…

\[ \frac{dU}{dT} = \tfrac{3}{2}nR = C_V n \rightarrow dU = C_V n \ dT. \]

Now we have an expresion for \(dU\) which we can relate to the first law of thermodynamics, remembering that heat transfer for an adiabatic process is zero:

\[ dU = Q - W_{by} = 0 - pdV = - pdV, \]

so we have

\[ C_V n \ dT = - pdV \rightarrow dT = \frac{pdV}{C_Vn}. \]

Now that we’ve related these two expressions for internal energy, let us consider the differential form of the ideal gas law:

\[ pV = nRT, \]
\[ Vdp + pdV = nRdT = - \frac{nRpdV}{C_Vn} = - \frac{RpdV}{C_V}. \]

Divide both sides by pV and recall that \(R = C_p - C_V\)…

\[ \frac{Vdp}{pV} + \frac{pdV}{pV} = - \frac{RpdV}{C_VpV}, \]
\[ \frac{dp}{p} + \frac{dV}{V} = - \frac{R \ dV}{C_VV} = - \frac{(C_p - C_V)}{C_V} \frac{dV}{V}. \]

Now, recall that:

\[ \gamma = 1 + \frac{R}{C_V} = 1 + \frac{C_p - C_V}{C_V}; \]

hence,

\[ \frac{dp}{p} + \frac{dV}{V} = - (\gamma - 1) \frac{dV}{V} = (1 - \gamma) \frac{dV}{V} = \frac{dV}{V} - \gamma \frac{dV}{V}. \]

The \(\tfrac{dV}{V}\) terms on either side cancel each other out through redundancy, and by adding the term on the RHS such that it equals zero, we are left with:

\[ \frac{dp}{p} + \gamma \frac{dV}{V} = 0. \]

Now, we are left with incomplete integrands. Recognise that when we integrate the RHS that we are left with a constant (as a constant differentiated becomes zero):

\[ \int{\frac{1}{p}dp} + \gamma \int{\frac{1}{V}dV} = \text{constant}, \]
\[ \ln{(p)} + \gamma \ln{(V)} = \text{constant}. \tag{4.32} \]

A sum of logs \(A\) and \(B\) is equivalent to a log of the product \(AB\). Knowing this, and the fact that \(a \times \log(B) = \log(B)^a\), we can rewrite the above equation as:

\[ \ln(pV^{\gamma}) = \text{constant}. \tag{4.33} \]

Finally, to remove the logarithm from the LHS we exponentiate. However, since \(e^{\text{constant}}\) is still a constant, we surmise that:

\[ pV^{\gamma} = \text{constant}, \tag{4.34} \]

and hence,

\[ p_1V_1^{\gamma} = p_2V_2^{\gamma} \]

for any adiabatic process.

5.13. 4.11 Work Done during a Reversible Adiabatic Process#

For an adiabatic process, no energy is transferred as heat \(Q = 0\). For a reversible (quasi-static) process, the work done by the gas is:

\[ W_{by} = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} p \, dV. \]

For an ideal gas undergoing a reversible adiabatic change, the equation of state is

\[ pV^\gamma = \text{const}. \]

We can therefore write the pressure as

\[ p = \frac{K}{V^\gamma}, \tag{4.35} \]

where \(K\) is a constant for this process.

Substituting into the expression for the work,

\[ W_{by} = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} \frac{K}{V^\gamma} \, dV = K \int_{V_1}^{V_2} V^{-\gamma} \, dV. \]

The integral evaluates to

\[ \int_{V_1}^{V_2} V^{-\gamma} \, dV = \left[\frac{V^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\right]_{V_1}^{V_2} \qquad (\gamma \neq 1), \]

so the work becomes

\[ W_{by} = \frac{K}{1-\gamma} \left( V_2^{1-\gamma} - V_1^{1-\gamma} \right). \tag{4.36} \]

Using \(K = pV^\gamma\), we evaluate the terms:

\[ K V_1^{1-\gamma} = p_1 V_1, \qquad K V_2^{1-\gamma} = p_2 V_2. \]

Substituting these into the expression for the work gives

\[ W_{by} = \frac{1}{1-\gamma} \left( p_2 V_2 - p_1 V_1 \right). \tag{4.37} \]

This is commonly written in the equivalent form

\[ \boxed{ W_{by} = \frac{p_1 V_1 - p_2 V_2}{\gamma - 1} } \tag{4.38} \]

which is obtained by multiplying the numerator and denominator of Eq. (4.37) by -1.

Sign of the work#

  • For an adiabatic compression (\(V_2 < V_1\)), the temperature rises and typically \(p_2 V_2 > p_1 V_1\), giving $\( W_{by} < 0, \)$ meaning work is done on the gas.

  • For an adiabatic expansion (\(V_2 > V_1\)), the work done by the gas is positive.
