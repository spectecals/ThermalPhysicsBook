## Thermodynamics of the Atmosphere

The Earth’s atmosphere can be viewed as a large thermodynamic system composed of a mixture of gases surrounding the planet and interacting continuously with solar radiation and the surface of the Earth. The temperature, pressure, and motion of the atmosphere determine weather patterns and regulate the global climate.

Understanding atmospheric behaviour requires the application of thermodynamic principles, including the ideal gas law, energy conservation, and radiative heat transfer.

The atmosphere is heated primarily by solar radiation and cooled by infrared radiation emitted back into space. The competition between these processes determines the global energy balance of the planet. Gravity produces strong vertical pressure gradients, while temperature differences across the Earth’s surface generate horizontal pressure gradients that drive winds and large-scale circulation.

## Composition and Vertical Structure of the Atmosphere

The Earth’s atmosphere is composed mainly of:
- Nitrogen (~78%)
- Oxygen (~21%)
- Trace gases (argon, carbon dioxide, water vapour, etc.)

Despite their small concentrations, trace gases can significantly affect thermodynamic behaviour due to their interaction with radiation.

The atmosphere is divided into layers based on temperature variation with altitude:

- Troposphere (~0–10/12 km): weather occurs here  
- Stratosphere  
- Mesosphere  
- Thermosphere

## Vertical Pressure Gradient

Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude due to the balance between gravity and pressure forces.

Hydrostatic equilibrium:

```{math}
\frac{dP}{dz} = -\rho g
```

where:
- \(P\): pressure  
- \(\rho\): density  
- \(g\): gravitational acceleration  

Using the ideal gas law:

```{math}
P = \rho R T
```

Combining these relations shows that pressure decreases approximately exponentially with altitude. This explains why pressure is much lower at high elevations than at sea level.

## Horizontal Pressure Gradients and Atmospheric Motion

Uneven solar heating creates horizontal pressure differences:

- Warmer regions → lower density → pressure differences  
- Air flows from high to low pressure → wind


Due to Earth’s rotation, moving air is deflected by the **Coriolis force**, causing rotation around low-pressure regions rather than direct inward flow.

## Radiation Balance of the Earth

The Earth’s temperature is determined by the balance between incoming solar radiation and outgoing thermal radiation.

Incoming energy per unit area:

```{math}
\frac{S(1 - \alpha)}{4}
```

where:
- \(S\): solar constant (~1360 W/m²)  
- \(\alpha\): albedo  

The factor \(1/4\) accounts for spherical geometry.

At equilibrium:

```{math}
\frac{S(1 - \alpha)}{4} = \sigma T^4
```

where:
- \(\sigma\): Stefan–Boltzmann constant  
- \(T\): effective temperature

## The Greenhouse Effect and Global Warming

The Earth’s actual surface temperature is higher than predicted by simple radiative equilibrium due to greenhouse gases:

- Water vapour  
- Carbon dioxide  
- Methane  
- Nitrous oxide  

These gases:
- Absorb infrared radiation from the surface  
- Re-emit radiation in all directions  
- Trap heat in the lower atmosphere  

This process is called the **greenhouse effect**.

Increasing greenhouse gas concentrations:
- Reduce heat loss to space  
- Shift radiative equilibrium  
- Increase surface temperature
